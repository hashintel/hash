A net composes components, subnets drawn once and instantiated many times,
by fusing places: a place of the net and ports of its instances become one
place when the net runs. This page is the proposal behind the code in
`petrinaut-core/src/composition/`, written as that code stands. The user
guide's Composition page says how the editor exposes it.

![Place fusion, drawn and flattened](./composition-fusion.d2)

## Why fusion, and why keep port arcs

The first version of subnets connects a component to its surroundings with
arcs only: a transition of the containing net reads from or writes to a port,
a place inside the instance, through a `componentPort` arc endpoint. That
works for a component used by one net, but it makes every connection pass
through a transition of the containing net, and two components can only
exchange tokens through such a transition. A buffer shared by two machines
needs a place of its own.

Place fusion is the classical answer: a fusion set names places that are one
place. Petrinaut's version keeps it small on purpose.

- A fusion belongs to the net that holds the instances. Its members are
  places of that net and ports of those instances, named the way an arc
  endpoint names them (`FusionMember = ArcEndpoint`), so the endpoint helpers
  and the id canonicalization apply unchanged.
- At most one member is a place of the net itself: the **anchor**. It gives
  the fused place its name, its initial marking, its visualizer, and the
  thing metrics and scenarios refer to. The other members are ports. A
  fusion without an anchor, two ports joined directly, makes a place that
  exists only inside the composition.
- Fusions are sets: a member belongs to one fusion, and a connection that
  joins two fusions merges them. `addFusion` and `addFusionMember` absorb a
  fusion that already holds one of the members; a member removed from a
  fusion of two members removes the fusion.
- Removing a place, an instance, a subnet, or a port's `isPort` mark takes
  it out of every fusion that named it, the way arcs on it go away.

Port arcs stay. A transition's arc to `Instance.Port` means the same thing
as an arc to the place that port is fused with: flattening resolves the port
to its scoped place and the fusion then maps that place onto the fused one,
so the two mechanisms compose. The two differ only in what the canvas shows,
a place of the net that the user can mark, watch and measure, against a port
reachable through its instance alone. Nothing forbids using both on one port.

## Ports

A port is a place of a component marked `isPort`. A port has no direction of
its own: it is a place, and a fusion may join it with any other place of the
right token type. What the checks read is how the component's transitions
touch it, a port they only consume from takes tokens in, one they only
produce into hands tokens on, and one no transition touches passes tokens
through for the net outside to use.

- `out` to `in`, so to speak, is the pipe. Two ports both only consumed from
  make a shared source that something else has to fill; two ports both only
  produced into make a place that accumulates. Both are allowed, and the
  dataflow checks say what is missing.
- Two ports of the same instance may be fused; it closes a loop through the
  component. Allowed, not reported.
- A port can be fused only with places and ports of the net that holds its
  instance. To reach a port of a nested instance from further out, the
  containing component declares a port of its own and fuses it with the
  nested port inside; the flattener follows the chain. The test in
  `flatten-component-instances.test.ts` named "applies fusions declared
  inside a subnet to the nested instances" shows the forwarding.

## What members must agree on

The fused place is one place, so its members must describe one place.
`checkFusions` reports, as errors, members that no longer resolve, a
duplicate member, a second anchor, a fusion of fewer than two members, a
transition that reaches the fused place twice, and the three disagreements:

- **Token type**: compared structurally, the same elements in the same
  order under the same names and value types, because a component's type and
  the net's type are different `Color` entities even when they say the same
  thing. The packed token layout is what has to match.
- **Capacity**: two declared capacities that differ. A declared capacity
  beside unbounded members bounds the fused place.
- **Dynamics**: two members whose dynamics compose different equations. One
  member's dynamics drive the fused place; two instances of one subnet share
  their equations and agree. The fused place runs them once, with the
  parameter values of the first member that has them.

A transition's code addresses its arcs by position, so two arcs of one kind
from one transition onto members of one fusion cannot be folded into a
single arc: that is the repeated-arc error, and the fix is to keep one of
them.

Two more conditions are reported below error severity: a fusion in which
every member only consumes gets a warning, nothing puts tokens into it; one
in which every member only produces gets a note, it accumulates. Arcs of the
containing net on the anchor, and port arcs on the member ports, count as
producers and consumers too.

Flattening throws on each of these errors too, apart from a duplicate member
and a fusion of fewer than two members, which merge nothing, so a document
the editor flags as broken does not simulate with a surprise.

## Flattening

`flattenComponentInstancesForSimulation` scopes every entity of an instance
under its id, `Instance::entity`. It collects each fusion, at each net it
walks, as a group of flat place ids, then `applyFusions` runs union-find
over the groups. The place with the fewest `::` separators leads its set, so
a root anchor keeps its id and its name, and the scoped ones merge into it.
Merging moves every arc onto the leader, takes a member's capacity onto the
leader, gives a leader with no dynamics the equations of the first member
that runs some, along with the parameter values they read, and drops the
merged places.

A fusion without an anchor keeps the first port's scoped place as the
leader, renamed after the fusion when it has a name, so a port-only fusion
reads as one place in the timeline. An anchor keeps its own name whatever
its fusion is called.

## Where the model stops today

- **Port directions**: a port could declare what its component promises,
  `in`, `out` or `inout`, checked against the arcs, drawn on its side of the
  instance, and the signature an interface would be made of.
- **Component libraries**: ready-made components a host or a plugin offers,
  copied into the document with a record of where they came from, and a
  root net marked as composed from components alone.
- **Initial markings** address root places. A component with internal
  state, a pool of workers say, has no marking of its own. A per-instance
  marking, or markings written into the component, is the next step.
- **Metrics** read root places. A port-only fusion is measurable only through
  the timeline's flattened places. Letting metrics name `Instance.Port`
  would follow the naming the arcs already use.
- **The GPU path** does not flatten instances yet, so a composed net runs on
  the CPU engine.
- **Clipboard**: copying instances copies no fusion. Fusions among copied
  instances could come along.
- **Interfaces**: binding an instance to an interface, and choosing
  an implementation per scenario, needs the port signature first.
- **Layout**: fusion links are edges for auto-layout, so fused nodes land
  near each other, but a port's side is not yet a layout constraint.
