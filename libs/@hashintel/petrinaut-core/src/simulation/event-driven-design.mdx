Quick Simulation today produces one frame per fixed time step `dt`. This
document proposes that it produces a **trace of events** instead: one record
per transition firing, carrying the firing time, the tokens it consumed and
produced, and the state after it. Continuous evolution between two events is
not stored; it is re-integrated on demand from the nearest recorded state.
Playback and the timeline move from frame indices to simulation time.
Backends that produce traces are chosen through the same assess-and-instantiate
contract experiments already use, so a plugin can provide one. Experiments keep
their `dt` observation grid, so metric frames, sharding and the GPU backend do
not change shape.

The document has four parts: what the engines do today and where `dt` is
assumed, the semantics and algorithms of the event-driven simulator, the
interfaces (trace, backend, playback, plugin contribution, constraint
monitors), and the proposed order of pull requests. The literature behind the
algorithm choices is in [Algorithms and tools](#algorithms-and-tools).

## What the engines do today

Two engines share compilation and step the same way; a third runs the same
semantics on the GPU. [Two execution paths](doc:two-execution-paths) covers
why the first two are separate. The facts below are what the event-driven
design has to replace or preserve.

### Semantics of one step

Both CPU engines (`engine/compute-next-frame.ts` for Quick Simulation,
`monte-carlo/advance-run.ts` for experiments) advance by a fixed `dt` in this
order, documented in [How a step is computed](doc:core.simulation.engine/stepping):

1. Dynamics: for each place with differential equations, every token's
   `real` attributes advance by one explicit Euler step of the sum of the
   place's composed equations. A one-token equation reads only that token's
   attributes and the net parameters; an equation over `n` tokens reads
   every combination of `n` tokens of the place (`HirDynamicsContext` in
   `hir/surface-context.ts`).
2. Transitions, in definition order. A transition is structurally enabled by
   its arc weights, inhibitors and output capacities. An enabled stochastic
   transition draws one uniform `U` and walks its input token combinations in
   lexicographic order; the first combination whose rate `λ` satisfies
   `exp(-λ · dt) ≤ U` fires. A predicate transition fires on the first
   combination whose guard is true. At most one firing per transition per
   step. Removals apply at once; additions apply at the end of the step.
3. Timers: `timeSinceLastFiringMs` advances by `dt` for every transition
   that did not fire and resets to zero for one that did.
4. Completion: `maxTime` reached, or no transition structurally enabled after
   a step with no firing.

The GPU backend (`webgpu/compile-net-shader/`) generates WGSL with the same
order, the same lexicographic scan, the same one-draw-per-transition rule, and
RK4 instead of Euler ([GPU backend](doc:core.webgpu/gpu-backend)).

experiment rows keep `dt` as an observation grid.

### What is already event-shaped

Actual mode (`actual-mode/types.ts`) renders an execution from a host as a
list of `ActualModeTransitionFiring { transitionId, input, output, ts }` plus
an initial marking, and builds its timeline from those firings and a fixed
tick. That is the event record this document proposes, minus the continuous
state. The execution-frame interface (`react/execution-frame/`) is the
indirection that let Actual mode reach the canvas without changes; the same
indirection carries the trace.

## Why fixed steps are wrong for this model

The engine is a fixed-step approximation of a continuous-time process. Each
item below is a defect of the approximation, with the evidence.

1. **Firing counts saturate.** A transition fires at most once per step with
   probability `1 - exp(-λ · dt)`, so its mean rate is at most `1 / dt`
   whatever `λ` says. A source at rate 20 with `dt` 0.1 produces at most 10
   per unit time. The error is controlled by `dt`, the input that
   [FE-1243](https://linear.app/hash/issue/FE-1243/remove-the-time-step-input-from-the-simulation-ui)
   asks to remove.
2. **Firing times are quantised to `dt`**, and simultaneous firings within a
   step are ordered by transition definition order. The first transition in
   the list wins every contested token.
3. **The combination rule under-approximates.** Each enabled token combination
   is a competing channel, so the transition's total rate is the sum over
   combinations. The engine tests each combination against one shared draw and
   fires the first that passes, which is correct for token-independent rates
   and biased otherwise. Decided semantics in
   [FE-1572](https://linear.app/hash/issue/FE-1572/fire-the-best-rate-token-combination-and-let-the-user-choose-a-places):
   sum the rates, choose a combination in proportion to its rate.
4. **Rates read state that is one Euler step old**, and Euler is first order.
   The CPU and GPU backends disagree by the integrator alone
   ([Simulation performance](doc:core.simulation/performance) measures Euler at
   0.15% and RK4 at 0.00155% error on logistic growth at `dt` 0.5).
5. **Guards fire late.** A predicate transition whose guard turns true at time
   `t` inside a step fires at the step end, up to `dt` later. A guard that
   turns true and false again within one step is never seen.
6. **Cost is proportional to simulated time, not to activity.** `maxTime`
   180 at `dt` 0.1 is 1800 steps of every transition per run, whether or not
   anything fires. [Simulation performance](doc:core.simulation/performance)
   estimates 10 to 100 times fewer steps for nets whose firings are sparse
   relative to `dt`.
7. **Every frame is a full snapshot**, retained three times on the interactive
   path ([Memory model](doc:core.simulation/memory-model)).

[FE-1499](https://linear.app/hash/issue/FE-1499/stochastic-transitions-fire-at-inflated-non-exponential-rates)
fixed the earlier inflated firing test; items 1 to 3 are what the per-step
Bernoulli test cannot fix.

## Target semantics

A Stochastic Dynamically Coloured Petri Net is a piecewise deterministic
Markov process: Everdij and Blom define the net class and prove the mappings
in both directions (see [Algorithms and tools](#algorithms-and-tools)). The
state is the marking, with each token's attributes. Between events the
marking is constant and each token's `real` attributes follow its place's
differential equation. Three kinds of event change the marking:

- A **delay transition** (today's `stochastic` lambda) fires at a random
  time. Each enabled token combination `c` is a channel with hazard
  `λ_c(x(t))`, a function of the chosen tokens' attributes and the
  parameters. The transition's total hazard is `Λ_T(t) = Σ_c λ_c(x(t))`. The
  probability that `T` has not fired by `t` is `exp(-∫ Λ_T)`. When it fires,
  combination `c` is chosen with probability `λ_c / Λ_T`.
- A **guard transition** (today's `predicate` lambda) fires as soon as some
  combination's guard is true, at that instant. A guard that is true at the
  moment the transition becomes enabled fires immediately.
- The **flow** itself never changes the marking. A guard that reads
  attributes can become true in the middle of a flow segment, which makes it a
  boundary-hitting event; its time is found by root-finding on the flow.

Simultaneous events get a **superdense time**: a pair `(t, k)` where `k`
counts the events at the same `t`. A firing at `(t, k)` sees the state after
`(t, k-1)`. Guard transitions are evaluated after every event at the same `t`,
in definition order, until none is enabled with a true guard; the chain is
bounded (a limit of iterations per instant, after which the run stops with an
error naming the transitions) because a net can be written that fires forever
at one instant.

Delay firings never coincide, since their times have continuous
distributions. Ties arise only among guard transitions at one instant.
Everdij and Blom's rules for that case are: a guard transition enabled by
several disjoint token combinations fires them all at once; where firing one
combination disables another, one is chosen uniformly at random; where
several transitions are enabled and firing one disables another, each
independently firable set gets the same probability. The simulator applies
definition order instead of the uniform choice between transitions, which is
today's rule and is what makes a guard chain reproducible without a second
random stream. The uniform choice between a transition's own combinations is
kept, drawn from that transition's stream.

A transition that is disabled and later re-enabled keeps its accumulated
hazard: the clock policy is age memory, in the vocabulary of generalised
stochastic Petri nets. It is what the bookkeeping of the next section gives
without special cases, and it is documented here because the alternative
policies (resampling on re-enablement, or resetting the clock) give different
distributions for the same net.

A run ends when `maxTime` is reached, when no transition is structurally
enabled and no token has dynamics that could enable one, or on a stopping
condition from [FE-1244](https://linear.app/hash/issue/FE-1244/make-stopping-conditions-an-obvious-input-when-starting-a-simulation).
A net whose only enabled transitions are delay transitions with `Λ_T = 0`
and no dynamics is deadlocked, and the simulator reports it rather than
waiting until `maxTime`. A net with dynamics and no enabled transition is not
deadlocked: a guard can still become true. The simulator integrates on, and
reports deadlock only when no guard reads an attribute that still moves.

Two existing behaviours are kept: removals of a firing apply before the next
transition at the same instant is evaluated, and output tokens are visible to
later events. The end-of-step batching of additions goes: with one firing per
event there is nothing to batch, and the capacity check sees true counts.

## The simulator

The run has two regimes and one loop. Nets without dynamics, which is most of
the examples, stay in the discrete regime for the whole run: no ODE solver is
constructed and the cost is per event. Nets with dynamics alternate between
flow segments and events.

### Discrete regime: next-reaction method

Every delay transition keeps a putative firing time. On a firing, only the
transitions whose input or output places changed are recomputed; the others
keep their times. This is the next-reaction method (Gibson and Bruck) with
Anderson's random-time-change bookkeeping, which is what lets the same
machinery handle time-varying hazards in the flow regime:

- Each delay transition `T` owns an internal clock `H_T` (its accumulated
  hazard) and a threshold `P_T` (the next point of a unit-rate Poisson
  process). `T` fires when `H_T` reaches `P_T`. After firing, `P_T` advances by
  a fresh `Exp(1)` draw. Transitions whose `Λ_T` changed keep `H_T` and `P_T`
  and only change the rate at which `H_T` grows.
- With constant `Λ_T` between events, the putative time is
  `t + (P_T - H_T) / Λ_T`, and a priority queue ordered by putative time gives
  `O(log |T|)` per event. A transition with `Λ_T = 0` has an infinite
  putative time and costs nothing until a dependency changes.
- The dependency graph is static: transition `T` depends on `T'` when they
  share an input place, or `T'` writes a place `T` reads (including inhibitor
  and read arcs and capacity-constrained outputs). It is computed at build
  time from the arcs, like `capacityConstraints` is today.

Computing `Λ_T` needs the rates of every enabled combination. Three cases, in
the order the engine tries them:

| Lambda reads                                    | `Λ_T`                                                             | Combination choice at firing                                               | Cost                                                                                                                   |
| ----------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| No input tokens (`readsNoInputTokens`, FE-1528) | `C(n, k) · λ` in closed form                                      | Uniform, or FIFO / LIFO by the place's out-order (FE-1572)                 | `O(1)`                                                                                                                 |
| Token attributes, few combinations              | Explicit sum over the lazy enumeration (FE-1526)                  | One pass of weighted reservoir sampling, or a second pass with one uniform | `O(C(n, k))` per recomputation                                                                                         |
| Token attributes, many combinations             | Same, with the enumeration-bound lint (FE-948) warning the author | Same                                                                       | Same, measured, with closed forms added where the HIR shape allows (product and affine forms have `O(n · k)` formulas) |

Today the sum is paid once per frame for every enabled transition. Under the
next-reaction method it is paid once per event for the transitions that
depend on it.

### Flow regime: integrate, accumulate hazards, locate events

When a place with dynamics holds tokens, the run integrates the flow between
events with an explicit adaptive Runge-Kutta pair with dense output
(Dormand-Prince 5(4) or Tsitouras 5(4); see
[Algorithms and tools](#algorithms-and-tools)). A token's derivative reads
only that token, so the ODE system is a set of independent small systems, one
per token, stepped together under one error norm.

The hazards ride along. For each delay transition whose rate reads an
attribute with dynamics, `H_T` is one more state component with derivative
`Λ_T(x(t))`, integrated by the same solver. The event `H_T = P_T` is located
by root-finding on the dense output (Illinois or Brent on the interpolant,
to a relative tolerance), as `ContinuousCallback` does in
DifferentialEquations.jl. Transitions whose rate does not read a moving
attribute keep the closed-form putative time from the discrete regime; the
integrator stops at the earliest of the next discrete putative time, the next
located flow event, the next reference frame, and `maxTime`.

Guards that read moving attributes are checked at every accepted step. A guard
that is false at the step start and true at the step end is located by
bisection on the dense output to the same tolerance; the located time becomes
a boundary event. Lowering the HIR comparison in a guard to a signed margin
(`a < b` to `b - a`) turns bisection into a proper root find and gives the
same signal the robustness semantics of
[Constraint monitors](#constraint-monitors) needs. The first increment uses
bisection on the boolean; the margin lowering is a later increment.

After any event the integrator restarts from the post-event state: the state
jumped, so the error estimate of a step across the event is meaningless, the
cached last stage is stale, and the step controller's memory is from the old
segment. The restart re-evaluates the derivative, picks the first step with
Hairer's initial-step heuristic, and excludes the event time itself from the
next root search so a located event does not fire twice (the discontinuity
sticking case). A cap on events per unit of time stops a net that chatters.
This restart is also what makes replay possible (next section).

The integrator is written in core. None of the JavaScript libraries surveyed
has dense output and event location together on a maintained code base, and
a Dormand-Prince pair with its interpolant and the ITP root finder is a few
hundred lines. A Rust port can take the `ode_solvers` crate, which has
Dopri5 with dense output and a stop hook.

`Λ_T` inside the right-hand side is the expensive case: the sum over
combinations is evaluated at every Runge-Kutta stage of every step. Two
mitigations, in order of preference: the closed forms above, and thinning
(Lewis and Shedler, Ogata) against a bound on `Λ_T` over the step, which
needs one lambda evaluation per proposal instead of one per stage. Thinning
needs a bound; the HIR cannot derive one for arbitrary user code, so it is
offered as a per-transition `maxRate` the author declares, with a run error
if the realised rate exceeds it. This is how JumpProcesses.jl's `Coevolve`
aggregator handles the same case.

### Stiffness

A per-token ODE with a large rate constant forces tiny steps: an explicit
pair's stability region bounds the step by about `3.3 / |λ_max|` whatever
the tolerance. The first release keeps explicit solvers and reports
stiffness: Hairer's estimate from the two last Dormand-Prince stages flags a
step when `h · ρ̃ > 3.25` for 15 consecutive steps, and the run shows the
place and the equation. A later release can switch a stiff place to a
Rosenbrock method (Rodas5P) with a Jacobian generated from the dynamics HIR;
the per-token systems are small enough that its LU per step costs little.
Tau-leaping and a fast-slow partition are the usual escape for stiff
stochastic regimes and are kept for experiments (next section), not for the
interactive trace.

### Prototype: the adaptive solver

The `adaptive` solver of the event-driven engine
(`event-trace/worker/adaptive-stepper.ts`, behind **Solver** in Simulation
Settings) is the first cut of the flow regime, built to measure before the
hazard integration above lands. One step spans at most the reference
interval and ends at a firing or at the span's end:

- **Firing times are sampled, not stepped.** At the span start every enabled
  token combination is a mode with its rate; the waiting time is exponential
  in the total rate and the firing mode is drawn in proportion. Rates are
  held at their span-start value, which is exact when no rate reads a
  moving attribute and a first-order approximation otherwise. A predicate
  that holds at the span start fires at once; one that starts to hold
  inside the span fires at the instant it does, located by bisection that
  re-integrates from the span start rather than by dense output.
- **Each place integrates by the class of its equation**, read off the
  dynamics HIR (`solver/classify-dynamics.ts`): a callback that reads only
  its own token is _independent_ and every token takes the steps its own
  dynamics need; one whose derivatives are affine in the token's real
  attributes with run-constant coefficients is _affine_ and advances exactly
  through the propagator `exp([[A, b], [0, 0]] h)`; anything else, the token
  index included, is _coupled_ and the place integrates as one system.
  Without the HIR every place is coupled.
- **Dormand-Prince 5(4)** with proportional step control (`solver/rk45.ts`)
  does the integrating, the user's **Max error** serving as both the
  relative and the absolute tolerance. Steps forced through at the smallest
  step the control allows, a discontinuous derivative's signature, are
  accepted and counted rather than failing the run.
- **Every event carries the accumulated local error per place**
  (the `solverError` entry of `TraceEvent.diagnostics`): the sum over accepted steps of the largest
  error estimate of any token's attribute, in attribute units. It is a
  running bound on what the steps themselves contributed, not the global
  error, which propagation through the dynamics can grow or shrink.

What the target design adds on top: hazards integrated with the state so
rates that read moving attributes stay exact, dense output for event
location, keyframes in place of full frames, and the stiffness report. The
buffer compiler accepts no dynamics that read another token today, so the
coupled class is reached only through the index parameter.

### Randomness

One counter-based generator (Philox, from Salmon et al. 2011, with 32-bit
integer arithmetic) keyed by `(seed, stream)` and indexed by draw number, so
the event-driven CPU backend, a WASM port and the WGSL shader can share
streams. Each transition owns a stream for its `Exp(1)` threshold draws, a
stream for its combination choices, and the kernel sink's distribution draws
take a stream per transition too; draws are indexed by purpose (the n-th
firing of transition `T`), never by call order, so adding a transition to a
net or changing a rate does not shift another transition's draws. The current
LCG in `engine/seeded-rng.ts` cannot be reproduced on the GPU and is already
different from the GPU's PCG. The reproducibility decision in
[Simulation performance](doc:core.simulation/performance) permits the change:
the same seed need not reproduce trajectories across engine versions.
Within one engine version and backend, the same `(net, marking, parameters,
seed, tolerance, maxTime)` reproduces the same trace.

Per-transition indexed streams also couple the event-driven engine to the
fixed-step one. In Kurtz's random time change representation, transition `T`
fires at the points of a unit-rate Poisson process read on its internal clock
`H_T`; the gaps between those points are the `Exp(1)` draws above. The
event-driven engine uses them as thresholds. A fixed-step engine that
advances `H_T` by `Λ_T · dt` per step and counts the thresholds crossed sees
the same Poisson path, so the two engines produce the same trajectory up to
discretisation error, which is what makes a parity test between them sharp.
The GPU keeps independent Poisson draws (next section) and agrees in
distribution only, which the `Dev / GpuParity` harness already measures
between CPU and GPU.

### Cost

| Net                                    | Fixed step, per run                        | Event-driven, per run                                                                                       |
| -------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Discrete only (SIR, supply chain)      | `(maxTime / dt) · \|T\|` evaluations       | `E · (log \|T\| + dependents)` where `E` is the number of firings                                           |
| Dynamics, rates read parameters only   | Same, plus Euler on every token every step | Adaptive steps between events (few, large steps when the flow is smooth) plus `E · log \|T\|`               |
| Dynamics, rates read moving attributes | Same                                       | Adaptive steps with `Λ_T` summed at every stage; the closed forms or `maxRate` decide whether this is cheap |

`E` can exceed `maxTime / dt`. SIR with 500 susceptible and 5 infected,
`maxTime` 60, `dt` 0.1 is 600 frames and up to 1010 firings. The event
engine then does more steps, each cheaper (one transition's effect against
every transition's evaluation), and the trace is exact. The sampled view
(next section) is how a consumer that wants 600 states gets them.

## The trace

A run is a sequence of event records and a way to get the state at any time.

```ts
type TraceTime = { time: number; index: number }; // superdense: (t, k)

type TraceEventKind = "initial" | "firing" | "reference" | "end";

type TraceEvent = {
  at: TraceTime;
  kind: TraceEventKind;
  /** Firings only. */
  transitionId?: string;
  /** Firings only: tokens removed per input place, by index into the pre-event state. */
  consumed?: readonly { placeIndex: number; tokenIndices: Uint32Array }[];
  /** Firings only: tokens added per output place, packed in the place's stride. */
  produced?: readonly { placeIndex: number; tokens: Uint8Array }[];
  /** The state after the event, in the engine frame format. Present on every
   *  keyframe; on other firings it is reconstructed from the previous keyframe. */
  frame?: ArrayBuffer;
};

type TraceStop = "maxTime" | "deadlock" | "livelock" | "error" | "cancelled";
```

The frame format is unchanged (`frames/internal-frame.ts`): the same readers
decode it, the string pool deltas travel the same way. The event carries what
the frame does not: time, the transition, and the effect.

**Keyframes.** A record with a `frame` is a keyframe; the others carry the
effect only. The store reconstructs a frame by applying the effects since the
last keyframe. The keyframe period is a store policy (every `K` events or
every `Δt`), hidden behind `getState`. A first increment may send a frame with
every firing, which is today's cost per frame moved to per event; a net with
thousands of tokens needs the keyframe policy before it needs anything else,
since a frame for 10,000 tokens of a three-field colour is about 240 KB.

**Reference events** are keyframes the flow regime emits without a firing,
when a segment has run for longer than a configured interval or more than a
configured number of solver steps. They bound the cost of replay inside a
long segment and cover places whose attributes move without any firing
touching them. A keyframe of a place with dynamics stores each token's
derivative next to its value: the solver has it at no cost, and cubic
Hermite interpolation between two keyframes is fourth order, so a consumer
that only needs display accuracy between two nearby keyframes interpolates
instead of re-integrating. Storing the solver's own step endpoints as
keyframes would make every interpolation exact to tolerance; at 16 bytes per
real field per step it is the right policy for a short run and the wrong one
for a long run with many tokens, so it is a store policy, like the keyframe
period.

**States between events.** `getState(t)` for `t` inside a segment finds the
last record at or before `t` by binary search, takes the nearest keyframe at
or before it, applies effects up to it, and re-integrates the flow from that
state to `t` with the run's solver settings. The post-event state at each
firing is recorded exactly by the live run, so replay never has to reproduce
the live run's event location; it integrates a segment whose two endpoints
are known. The live run and the replay start every segment from the same
state with the same tolerances, so the replayed values differ from the live
ones only in the last step before `t`, within the tolerance. Replayed values
feed the canvas, the timeline and the monitors in display mode; they never
feed a rate. Nets without dynamics skip the integration: the state between
events is the state after the last one.

**Left and right limits.** The state just before a firing at `(t, k)` is the
state after `(t, k-1)`, or the flow at `t` from the previous segment when
`k = 0`. Both are reachable through `getState({ time, index })`, which is
what a monitor needs to see a jump as two samples at one time. This is the
convention of DifferentialEquations.jl's `save_positions = (true, true)`
and of Modelica result files, which hold two rows at an event time. Its
`SSAStepper` saves the post-jump state only, because a piecewise-constant
signal is determined by its right limits; the trace records post-event
states for the same reason and derives the left limit.

**Sampled views.** A consumer that wants states on a grid asks for a range
and a period: `sampleStates({ from, to, period })`. The backend answers in
one batch, re-integrating each segment once for all samples that fall inside
it. This is how the timeline draws a series, how playback at a given speed
gets its next second of frames, and how experiments observe on their `dt`
grid. StochPy keeps the jump list and resamples it on request the same way,
and AnyLogic offers its datasets either at a recurrence time or at events;
the trace keeps events and makes the grid a view.

## Interfaces

### Simulation backend contract

The experiment side already has the contract this needs
([Backend selection](doc:core.experiments/backend-selection)): a plain request,
`assess` without acquiring resources, `instantiate` returning a handle,
refusal as a value, registrations with deferred loading, selection in
preference order. The simulation side gets the same shape.

```ts
type SimulationRequest = {
  sdcpn: SDCPN;
  extensions?: PetrinautExtensionSettings;
  initialMarking: InitialMarking;
  parameterValues: Readonly<Record<string, string>>;
  seed: number;
  stop: { maxTime: number | null };
  solver: {
    /** Relative and absolute tolerances of the flow integrator. */
    rtol: number;
    atol: number;
    /** Longest flow segment without a reference keyframe. */
    referenceInterval: number;
  };
  hirArtifacts?: HirArtifacts;
};

type SimulationBackend = {
  readonly id: string;
  readonly label: string;
  readonly needsHirTrees: boolean;
  isAvailable(this: void): boolean;
  assess(this: void, request: SimulationRequest): Promise<SimulationAssessment>;
  dispose?(this: void): void;
};

type SimulationHandle = {
  readonly status: ReadableStore<SimulationState>;
  /** Count and latest record; the store keeps the records. */
  readonly events: ReadableStore<{ count: number; latest: TraceEvent | null }>;
  readonly stop: ReadableStore<TraceStop | null>;
  run(this: void): void;
  pause(this: void): void;
  /** Lets the backend compute up to `at`; replaces frame acks. */
  demand(this: void, at: TraceTime): void;
  getEvent(this: void, index: number): TraceEvent | null;
  getState(
    this: void,
    at: TraceTime | number,
  ): Promise<SimulationFrameReader | null>;
  sampleStates(
    this: void,
    range: { from: number; to: number; period: number },
  ): Promise<SimulationFrameReader[]>;
  dispose(this: void): void;
};
```

`dt` is gone from the request. The fixed-step backend that wraps today's
engine takes its step from a backend option at construction, the same way
the GPU backend takes its ODE method. `demand` replaces `ack`: the consumer
names the time it wants computed, the backend runs until its trace covers it
and then waits, which is the backpressure contract of
[Worker protocol and backpressure](doc:core.simulation.worker/protocol) with time in
place of frame counts.

The worker protocol gains `events` batches in place of `frames`, a
`getState` request and response, and `sampleStates`. The transport, the
in-memory store and the status stores keep their shape; `createSimulation`
becomes the host-side runtime behind the built-in backends, as
`createMonteCarloExperiment` is behind the worker-pool backend.

### Built-in backends

| Backend        | Produces                                                                                                                                                                                                        | Status                                     |
| -------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------ |
| `fixed-step`   | Events from the current engine's frames: one firing record per transition that fired in a step, in order, all at the step's time; a reference keyframe every `K` steps; states between keyframes by re-stepping | Wraps the existing engine; first increment |
| `event-driven` | The simulator of [The simulator](#the-simulator)                                                                                                                                                                | New                                        |

The `fixed-step` wrapper is what lets every consumer move to the trace before
the new simulator exists, which is step 1 of
[FE-1247](https://linear.app/hash/issue/FE-1247/event-driven-simulation).
Its states between events are the engine's own frames, so its sampled view
is exact at multiples of `dt` and piecewise constant between them.

### Playback by time

`Playback` (`playback/playback.ts`) holds a time instead of a frame index.
`tick` advances the time by elapsed wall-clock time times the speed, clamped
to the trace's covered time; "Max" speed jumps to the latest event. Two new
controls step to the previous and next event. The provider resolves the
displayed state through `sampleStates` one display second ahead, and through
`getState` on a scrub. `playback-time.ts` prints the trace time at a
precision set by the time unit
([FE-1822](https://linear.app/hash/issue/FE-1822/set-playback-time-in-days-weeks-or-months)),
since times are no longer multiples of a step.

`ExecutionFrameSource` becomes an `ExecutionTraceSource`:

```ts
type ExecutionTraceSource = {
  sourceId: string;
  events: ReadableStore<{ count: number; latest: TraceEvent | null }>;
  coveredTime: ReadableStore<number>;
  current: { at: TraceTime; reader: SimulationFrameReader | null };
  scrubTo(at: TraceTime | number): void;
  getState(at: TraceTime | number): Promise<SimulationFrameReader | null>;
  sampleStates(range: {
    from: number;
    to: number;
    period: number;
  }): Promise<SimulationFrameReader[]>;
};
```

The canvas reads `current`. The timeline draws the sampled series and marks
events on the time axis. The transition readout `firedInThisFrame` becomes
"fired at the current event", and `timeSinceLastFiringMs` becomes the time
since the transition's last event, computed from the trace rather than
stored in the frame.

Actual mode implements the same source from its recording: its firings are
events, its initial marking is the initial keyframe, and it has no flow, so
`getState` is the marking after the last firing at or before `t`. The
adapter in `actual-mode/timeline.ts` that turns firings into timeline points
goes.

### Plugin-provided backends

The plugin manifest ([Plugin API reference](doc:ui.plugins/reference)) gains a
`backends` key, and the hook returns one registration per declared key:

```ts
definePetrinautPlugin(
  {
    id: "acme.rust-simulator",
    name: "Rust simulator",
    backends: {
      rust: { label: "Rust (WASM)", kinds: ["simulation", "experiment"] },
    },
  },
  (api) => ({
    backends: {
      rust: {
        simulation: { load: () => import("./simulation-backend") },
        experiment: { load: () => import("./experiment-backend") },
      },
    },
  }),
);
```

The registration id is `<pluginId>.<key>`, recorded against results like
`ExperimentRecord.computeBackend` is today. The simulation and experiment
providers read registrations from one context that merges the built-in ones
with the running plugins' contributions; the Compute backend setting lists
them all. The selection walk is unchanged: the user's choice first, then the
fallbacks, with every refusal recorded. A plugin backend that returns a
refusal with `origin: "model"` has its reason shown against the net item.

A plugin backend can run anywhere a request can be sent: its own worker, a
WASM module, or a remote process over a WebSocket, since both request types
are structured-cloneable by construction. Möbius's abstract functional
interface is the precedent: every solver implements one contract over the
model, and formalisms and solvers are added without editing each other.
Three helpers in core make writing a backend tractable:

- `encodeEngineFrame(layout, { counts, tokens })` and the existing
  `encodeTokenToBytes`, so a backend produces frames the readers decode
  without knowing byte offsets. Actual mode already encodes its markings this
  way.
- `hirArtifacts` with trees when the backend declares `needsHirTrees`, so a
  backend compiling to its own target has the lowered user code. The Rust
  prototype's scalar HIR to WASM emitter
  ([hashintel/hash#10213](https://github.com/hashintel/hash/pull/10213)) is
  such a consumer.
- The counter-based generator's reference implementation and test vectors,
  so a backend that wants shared streams can match them.

### Experiments

Experiments keep the `dt` observation grid: a metric frame per grid time, a
shard watermark per grid index, and the GPU backend as it is. The grid is an
input of the experiment, which is what `ExperimentRequest.dt` already is;
renaming it `samplePeriod` says what it does.

Two changes improve experiments without changing their shape:

1. **Firing counts per step.** The fixed-step CPU and GPU engines replace the
   one-firing Bernoulli test with a count: `Binomial(N_T, Λ_T · dt / N_T)`
   firings, where `N_T` is the enabling degree (the most firings the input
   places allow), consuming tokens in the decided combination order. The
   binomial form (Tian and Burrage) never consumes more than the places hold;
   it tends to `Poisson(Λ_T · dt)` when `N_T` is large. This removes the
   saturation in
   [Why fixed steps are wrong](#why-fixed-steps-are-wrong-for-this-model),
   item 1, at a cost of one count draw per enabled transition per step. With
   `dt` chosen so that enablement changes little within a step, this is
   tau-leaping, whose error is first order in `dt` (Rathinam et al.); the
   step-size rule of Cao, Gillespie and Petzold gives the `dt` for a target
   relative change and can be reported as the run's error estimate. The CPU
   engine draws the count by crossing thresholds on the shared Poisson path
   (previous section); the GPU draws a binomial by inversion for small
   `N_T · p` and by the transformed rejection method above that. Either
   engine can place a firing inside the step rather than at its end by
   interpolating `H_T` across the step and integrating the remainder from
   the located time, which removes the `dt` delay of grid-aligned firings at
   the cost of one more stage.
2. **The event-driven engine as an experiment backend.** The worker-pool
   backend gains an option to run the event-driven simulator and observe on
   the grid through `sampleStates`. Metric specs, accumulators and shard
   merging do not change, since they consume readers at grid times. A net
   without dynamics then runs exact trajectories at a cost proportional to
   its firings.

The GPU stays fixed-step. Per-thread event-driven stepping is possible (the
DiffEqGPU per-thread adaptive kernels do it), but threads in a workgroup
would diverge on event counts and step counts, and the histogram reduction
is per grid frame, which an event-driven kernel would have to reconstruct.
The Poisson count change gives the GPU most of the accuracy gain.

### Constraint monitors

The constraints planned for Petrinaut are Signal Temporal Logic formulas over
real-valued signals (the evaluation in the Petrinaut Notion space settled on
STL, with quantitative robustness so an optimiser can use the margin). A
monitor for one formula is an accumulator that rides with the run and reports
satisfaction, and a margin, online. The existing state constraints are the
degenerate case: a boolean metric with `aggregateTime: "min"` is
`G(p)` evaluated at grid times.

The monitor contract extends the metric accumulator monoid
(`monte-carlo/metrics/accumulators.ts`) with time:

```ts
type MonitorSegment = {
  from: TraceTime;
  to: TraceTime;
  /** Signal values at `from` and `to`; piecewise constant for counts,
   *  linear (or Hermite with the recorded derivative) for attributes. */
  start: Float64Array;
  end: Float64Array;
};

type ConstraintMonitor<State> = {
  empty(): State;
  observe(state: State, segment: MonitorSegment): State;
  /** Satisfaction so far, with the robustness margin; `undefined` while a
   *  bounded-future operator still waits for its horizon. */
  read(state: State): { holds: boolean; margin: number } | undefined;
  merge?(left: State, right: State): State;
};
```

The trace supports this contract in four ways:

- Superdense times let a monitor see the value before and after a firing at
  one time. A record at `(t, k > 0)` has zero duration and feeds only the
  edge-sensitive operators; the dense-time semantics the monitors use
  evaluates `F_[a,b]` over simulation time, where a discrete-time monitor fed
  event-timed records would read it as "within `b` records".
- Segments carry durations and endpoint values, so the robustness of a
  predicate over a segment is exact for counts (piecewise constant) and
  within the solver tolerance for attributes (piecewise linear, or Hermite
  where the derivative is recorded). Abbas et al. show the robustness value
  depends on the reconstruction between samples. The derivative recorded
  at keyframes keeps that reconstruction within the solver tolerance.
- Past-time operators compile to constant registers, one value and one
  timestamp per operator (Havelund and Roşu's construction, extended to
  metric intervals as Reelay does). Bounded-future operators `G_[a,b]` and
  `F_[a,b]` compile to a sliding-window minimum or maximum over a buffer
  sized by the horizon divided by the shortest segment (Deshmukh et al.'s
  robust satisfaction interval, with Lemire's filter over the window). A
  shortest segment exists because the simulator coalesces records closer
  than a configured minimum into one superdense group.
- The guard margin lowering of [The simulator](#the-simulator) gives the
  atomic predicates their signed value, so the margin and the guard share
  one lowering.

On the GPU the same monitors are fixed-size ring buffers per run, one per
bounded operator, sized by the horizon divided by the grid period; the grid
observation is what makes them fixed-size, and a block prefix-suffix maximum
(van Herk) replaces the deque where branches cost. A monitor with an
unbounded horizon reads at the end of the run, as the `min` aggregation does
today. Across runs, the fraction that holds is what the sweep's pass
threshold already reports; sequential tests (the SPRT, as UPPAAL SMC and
PRISM run them) let an experiment stop early once the fraction is settled.

The compile step takes a formula to a monitor, in the HIR pipeline that
already compiles metrics, so the CPU engines, the GPU shader and a WASM
backend each get one from the same lowering, the way RTLola and TeSSLa
compile stream equations to several targets. The formulas themselves, the
constraint editor and the optimiser's use of the margin are outside this
document.

## Algorithms and tools

What the design takes from each source, with the alternatives it does not
take and why.

### Stochastic simulation

| Method                                                                             | Per-event cost                                 | Rates varying between events   | Taken                                                                                                     |
| ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Direct method (Gillespie 1976, 1977)                                               | `O(\|T\|)`: sum and linear search              | No                             | The total-rate formulation, for the combination choice within a transition                                |
| First reaction method (Gillespie 1976)                                             | `O(\|T\|)` draws                               | Yes, per transition            | The race semantics (one clock per transition)                                                             |
| Next reaction method (Gibson and Bruck 2000)                                       | `O(d · log \|T\|)`, `d` dependents             | With hazard integration        | The indexed priority queue and the static dependency graph                                                |
| Modified next reaction method (Anderson 2007)                                      | `O(d · log \|T\|)`, one `Exp(1)` per event     | Yes, by integrating the hazard | The bookkeeping: internal clocks and thresholds per transition, age memory for free                       |
| Composition-rejection (Slepoy et al. 2008)                                         | `O(1)` in `\|T\|` with bounded rate range      | No                             | Only if nets reach thousands of transitions                                                               |
| Thinning (Lewis and Shedler 1979; Ogata 1981)                                      | One lambda evaluation per proposal             | Yes, with a rate bound         | The opt-in `maxRate` path for expensive variable rates, as JumpProcesses.jl's `Coevolve` does             |
| Hazard integration with the ODE (Haseltine and Rawlings 2002; Alfonsi et al. 2005) | One ODE component per variable-rate transition | Yes, exact to tolerance        | The default for variable rates: no bound needed, and the solver's root finder locates the event           |
| Random time change (Kurtz 1980)                                                    |                                                |                                | The representation that couples the event engine and the fixed-step engine on one Poisson path            |
| Tau-leaping (Gillespie 2001; Cao, Gillespie, Petzold 2006; Tian and Burrage 2004)  | `O(\|T\|)` per leap, any number of firings     | Frozen over the leap           | The fixed-step engines' firing counts (binomial, bounded by the enabling degree) and their error estimate |
| Slow-scale SSA (Cao, Gillespie, Petzold 2005)                                      |                                                |                                | Not taken: it needs the fast subsystem's equilibrium moments, which user code does not give               |

Everdij and Blom (2005, 2010) define the dynamically coloured Petri net,
prove the mappings to and from piecewise deterministic Markov processes
(Davis 1984), and state the rules for simultaneous firings that
[Target semantics](#target-semantics) adopts and restricts. Generalised
stochastic Petri nets (Ajmone Marsan, Conte and Balbo 1984) are the source
of the immediate-transition priority and of the vocabulary of clock policies.
Superdense time is Ptolemy II's (Lee and Zheng 2005) and Modelica's event
iteration reaches the same fixed point.

### Continuous dynamics

| Topic                 | Choice                                                                                                | Source                                                                              |
| --------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Integrator            | Dormand-Prince 5(4) with the first-same-as-last stage, or Tsitouras 5(4)                              | Dormand and Prince 1980; Tsitouras 2011; Hairer, Nørsett and Wanner, Solving ODEs I |
| Step control          | Hairer's Lund-stabilised controller (`β` 0.04, safety 0.9, factors 0.2 to 10), or Söderlind's PI form | `dopri5.f`; Söderlind 2003                                                          |
| First step            | Hairer's heuristic from the scaled norms of `y` and `f`, run after every event                        | Solving ODEs I, II.4                                                                |
| Dense output          | Shampine's fourth-order interpolant for Dormand-Prince, or cubic Hermite from `(y, f)` at step ends   | Shampine 1986                                                                       |
| Event location        | Sign change over the step, then ITP on the interpolant; the root just before the crossing             | Shampine and Thompson 2000; Oliveira and Takahashi 2020                             |
| Restart and sticking  | Re-evaluate `f`, new first step, exclude the left endpoint                                            | Park and Barton 1996; the CVODE and DifferentialEquations.jl rules                  |
| Stiffness             | Hairer's estimate from the last two stages; Rosenbrock later                                          | Solving ODEs II, IV.2; Steinebach 2023                                              |
| SDE attributes, later | Milstein or SRI for diagonal noise, with a virtual Brownian tree so the path does not depend on steps | Rößler 2010; Li et al. 2020; Jelinčič et al. 2024                                   |
| GPU                   | Fixed step, integer step counter for time, compensated sums for `H_T`                                 | Utkarsh et al. 2024 (DiffEqGPU)                                                     |

Quantised-state solvers (Kofman 2001) integrate an ODE as a stream of
events per state variable and would make the flow itself event-shaped. They
are not taken: the flow here is many small independent systems, and an
adaptive pair with dense output gives the same frame-at-any-time property
with less machinery.

### Monitors

Signal temporal logic is Maler and Nickovic 2004; its robustness semantics
Fainekos and Pappas 2009 and Donzé and Maler 2010; the offline algorithm
over piecewise-linear signals Donzé, Ferrère and Maler 2013, with Lemire's
2006 filter for bounded operators; online monitoring with bounded horizons
Deshmukh et al. 2017. Havelund and Roşu 2002 give the constant-space
past-time construction; Reelay (Ulus) implements it over dense time with
delta-encoded inputs; RTAMT (Ničković and Yamaguchi 2020) restricts online
monitoring to bounded-future formulas; RTLola and TeSSLa compile stream
equations with static memory bounds to several targets; STLCG++ (2025)
computes robustness in batch on a GPU. Ouaknine and Worrell 2007 bound what
is decidable: unbounded-future metric formulas over infinite words are not,
so the monitors take past-time and bounded-future formulas only.

## Decisions to take

1. Whether a firing record carries a full frame from the first increment, or
   keyframes from the start. The document recommends full frames in
   increment 2 with the keyframe policy in the same store interface, and
   measuring a 10,000-token net before increment 7.
2. Whether experiments adopt the summed combination rule and Poisson counts
   (increment 5) before or after the event-driven backend exists. The
   document recommends before: it is the change that fixes rate saturation
   for every backend, and it changes seeded trajectories once rather than
   twice.
3. The default tolerances and reference interval, to be set from the bundled
   examples with the parity harness.
4. Whether the event-driven backend's first target is TypeScript on the
   existing buffer programs (this document) or the WASM emitter of the Rust
   prototype. The TypeScript target reuses the compiled lambdas, kernels and
   dynamics as they are and leaves the port as a later backend behind the
   same contract.
5. The time unit and its display precision (FE-1822), which playback by time
   needs in increment 3.
