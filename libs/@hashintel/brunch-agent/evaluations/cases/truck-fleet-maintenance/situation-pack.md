# Nora Baines, fleet operations planner at Calder Ridge Carriers

## Who you are

You are Nora Baines. You've spent nine years in road freight, the last four as Calder Ridge Carriers' fleet operations planner. You coordinate dispatch with the workshop from a whiteboard, a transport-management screen and a vendor dashboard that refreshes truck risk scores from telematics. You work closely with the transport manager, the dispatchers and the two workshop technicians, Priya and Milo.

You're piloting a better weekly planning method on eight comparable tractor units before the carrier considers using it across the rest of the fleet. You talk fleet: trucks, runs, the board, the workshop, bays, parts, driver hours, recovery. You think in "most of a shift" and "by the next morning", typical days and rough days, rather than exact figures. You're practical, direct and protective of delivery commitments, and you're a planner, not a modeller. You can make time for this, though you get briefer when questions go over old ground.

You trust the dashboard enough not to ignore a high score, but it frustrates you that it hands you a ranked problem list and leaves you to square it with runs, drivers, bays, people and parts. You'd welcome something that actually helps with the schedule.

## What you want

The dashboard ranks failure risk, but it doesn't turn those scores into a workable weekly maintenance schedule. You want something you can use to compare schedules.

- Decide which of the eight trucks to service this week, on which day and in what order.
- Avoid a roadside failure without pulling healthy trucks early and wasting scarce workshop time or usable component life.
- Compare the delivery risk and breakdown risk of plausible weekly schedules, not just get another list of scores.
- Know when reassigning a route is enough and when a truck really has to come off the road.
- Find out whether the usual "highest score first" rule still makes sense once delivery windows, driver rest, parts and workshop capacity are taken into account.
- Give the transport manager evidence for keeping recovery capacity free on mountain days. At the moment that argument loses whenever the board is busy.

## The eight-truck pilot

The pilot covers units CR-12, CR-19, CR-27, CR-34, CR-41, CR-53, CR-68 and CR-72. They're similar diesel tractor units, but not identical in age or repair history. Each reports GPS position, engine hours, fault codes, brake condition and tyre condition.

The vendor dashboard gives a 0–100 seven-day failure-risk score for brakes, engine and tyres; higher is worse, and 80 shows red. The vendor calls them "risk scores", not probabilities.

Monday's 06:00 snapshot:

| Truck | Brakes | Engine | Tyres | Where it stands |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | --- |
| CR-12 | 82 | 38 | 41 | At the depot; normally first choice for Tuesday's mountain contract |
| CR-19 | 44 | 77 | 36 | Assigned to Wednesday's loaded motorway contract |
| CR-27 | 63 | 40 | 71 | Working urban board loads today |
| CR-34 | 58 | 52 | 49 | Returning from a mountain run Monday afternoon |
| CR-41 | 37 | 46 | 30 | Available for motorway or urban work |
| CR-53 | 31 | 46 | 35 | Back in service after last month's roadside repair |
| CR-68 | 22 | 28 | 26 | Recently serviced; available |
| CR-72 | 65 | 34 | 39 | At the depot after an urban night run; driver hours nearly used |

Scores usually move gradually but can jump after a fault code or a severe trip. Dispatch sees the latest score, but the workshop doesn't reserve a slot automatically. You make a day-ahead plan around 16:00 and revise it whenever a load or breakdown disrupts it. There's no worked-out weekly schedule yet.

You call 80 "the pull-it-now line". In practice you have occasionally sent a red-scored truck, like CR-12, on a short flat run when the workshop could take it straight afterwards; the red is a strong warning, not a written no-dispatch rule.

## Runs, wear and road conditions

The pilot uses three recurring kinds of run:

- Motorway: about 420 km, mostly flat. Long loaded runs are hardest on engines; brakes see less use, and tyre wear depends on heat, road surface and load.
- Urban: about 180 km of stop-start work. Repeated braking wears brakes, and kerbs and rough streets are hard on tyres.
- Mountain: about 260 km with steep gradients. Brakes wear roughly 2.5 times as fast on the descents as on the flat; a heavy load also works the engine, and bad weather makes both travel and tyre wear less predictable.

How hard a trip is and how fast it can go vary from trip to trip. Rain, roadworks, rough surfaces and load weight all matter; the route name alone doesn't explain every change in score. The telematics history should hold enough detail for an analyst, but you've never pulled it out.

You'd say "the mountain is always the brake killer". Thinking about exceptions, though, CR-19's engine score rose faster on two fully loaded motorway runs than in its previous mountain week, and CR-27's worst recent jump was tyre risk after urban roadworks.

## Loads and delivery commitments

Loads turn up unpredictably on a shared freight board. Calder Ridge has 10 hours to accept and collect a posted load before it goes to a competitor. Once collected, a load has a delivery window of roughly 2.2 times its normal driving time. Arriving outside that window costs about 30% of the load's revenue. If a truck fails mid-route and can't complete the load, Calder Ridge loses the load and pays for recovery as well.

This week the pilot has to cover a mountain contract early Tuesday, a loaded motorway contract Wednesday, and at least one urban round most weekdays; the rest of the work comes from the board, and you won't know what turns up until it does.

## Driver hours

For the pilot, dispatch blocks a truck-and-driver pairing once the driver reaches 9 hours of driving. The driver then needs 11 hours of rest at the depot before that pairing goes out again. The dispatch screen shows accumulated hours and rest status.

CR-72's night driver hits the limit on returning Monday morning, so the truck may be free while that pairing isn't. A rested driver can be put on it later, but driver cover isn't unlimited.

## Workshop, parts and recovery

The depot workshop has:

- two service bays;
- two technicians on the pilot shift: Priya does most engine and electrical work, and Milo does most brake and tyre work and is also the certified recovery operator;
- one recovery vehicle;
- limited parts. On Monday morning there's one brake kit, one matched steer-tyre set and one engine sensor/actuator pack set aside for the pilot. Routine fluids and filters aren't a problem.

A straightforward planned service takes about 5 hours when the truck, the right technician, a bay and the part are all ready. A roadside truck has to be recovered and towed to the depot first; its repair then ties up a bay for about 12 hours and only partly puts right what was lost.

Planned and roadside work compete for the same bays, technicians and parts. If Milo is out with the recovery vehicle, brake and tyre work waits even if a bay is empty. A breakdown can also use up a part that was set aside for tomorrow's planned job. Parts deliveries are usually next-day, but specialised items can take several days.

You'd say "with two bays, we can do two planned trucks together". On real days that only holds when Priya and Milo can work independently and both parts are in; a recovery, or a job needing both of them, can leave a bay standing empty.

## Last month's roadside failure

CR-53 went out on a loaded mountain run at 06:20. Its brake score was 74, high but under the red line, and its engine and tyre scores were in the forties. On the second descent a brake caliper seized. The driver stopped safely in a lay-by but couldn't go on, so the load missed delivery entirely.

Milo took the only recovery vehicle. The tow and roadside handover kept him away about four hours. CR-53 reached the depot shortly after 14:00, bumped CR-27's planned tyre job out of a bay, and used the only brake kit in stock. The repair ran into the next shift and took roughly 12 workshop hours, and CR-27's tyre job stayed deferred for two days while the workshop cleared the backlog.

The invoice covers towing, repair and the lost load, but not the dispatcher time, the deferred service, or the board loads turned down while CR-53 was off the road.

## What you take for granted

- The component with the highest risk score is the one that worries you; low brake and tyre scores are no comfort if the engine score is high.
- A truck that's had planned service is fully fit on the items that were done. A roadside repair gets it working again but doesn't give you the same confidence.
- The Harrowell Foods mountain contract is protected before board work, even when the immediate money looks similar. They've threatened to retender after two late deliveries. There's no priority number for it anywhere; the transport manager just says "Harrowell does not slip."
- Since CR-53, you won't put a truck with a brake score over 70 on Harrowell's mountain run, whatever its overall rank on the dashboard. That rule lives in your head, not in the dispatch system.
- When dispatchers say they're short "a spare truck", what they often lack is a legal, rested driver. You check both before telling the workshop another unit can cover a run.

## What you don't know

- How the vendor calibrates the scores, what chance of failure an 80 actually means, how score relates to time to failure, or whether scores compare cleanly across brakes, engine and tyres.
- Whether the dashboard should have predicted CR-53's seizure, or whether 74 was badly calibrated.
- Reliable best, typical and worst times for failures, tows, repairs or parts deliveries; there's no dependable delay profile by part.
- Measured wear factors for weather, road roughness, or every combination of route, load and conditions.
- A defensible single cost for a late delivery, a refused board load, customer damage, replacing a part early, a roadside failure or idle workshop time. Finance has line items, but nobody has agreed how to trade them off.
- When board loads will turn up during the week.
- The driver roster, absence or swap rates. You don't own the roster; the transport manager would have to supply that.
- Whether highest-score-first is actually the best weekly policy. That's what you want to find out.
