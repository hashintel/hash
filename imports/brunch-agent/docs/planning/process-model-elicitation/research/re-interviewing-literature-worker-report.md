# Requirements Elicitation by Interview: Literature Report

## 1. Interview-based RE guidance and technique selection

### Zowghi & Coulin 2005 (read in full, camera-ready copy)
**Zowghi, D. & Coulin, C., "Requirements Elicitation: A Survey of Techniques, Approaches, and Tools," ch. 2 in Aurum & Wohlin (eds.), *Engineering and Managing Software Requirements*, Springer, 2005, pp. 19–46. DOI 10.1007/3-540-28244-0_2.** (Full chapter read via https://web.eecs.umich.edu/~weimerw/2022-481W/readings/requirements.pdf)

- **Five fundamental elicitation activities** (§2.2.1): (1) understanding the application domain, (2) identifying the sources of requirements, (3) analyzing the stakeholders, (4) selecting the techniques, approaches, and tools to use, (5) eliciting the requirements from stakeholders and other sources. Plus five analyst roles: facilitator, mediator, documenter, proxy, validator.
- ⚠️ **Premise correction:** the familiar six-category taxonomy (traditional/group/prototyping/model-driven/cognitive/contextual) is **not** theirs — it is **Nuseibeh & Easterbrook, "Requirements Engineering: A Roadmap," ICSE 2000, pp. 35–46, DOI 10.1145/336512.336523**. Zowghi & Coulin list ~20 techniques (interviews, questionnaires, task analysis, domain analysis, introspection, repertory grids, card sorting, laddering, group work, brainstorming, JAD, workshops, ethnography, observation, protocol analysis, apprenticing, prototyping, goal-based approaches, scenarios, viewpoints) and reduce them to a **core eight** (interviews, domain analysis, groupwork, ethnography, prototyping, goals, scenarios, viewpoints) in two tables: Table 2.1 maps techniques × the five activities (interviews/domain/groupwork suit all five); Table 2.2 marks each pair Complementary vs Alternative (e.g., interviews complementary with goals/scenarios/viewpoints, alternative to groupwork/ethnography/prototyping).
- On **interviews**: "probably the most traditional and commonly used technique"; three types — unstructured ("risk that some topics may be completely neglected"; best "for exploration when there is a limited understanding of the domain, or as a precursor to more focused and detailed structured interviews"), structured ("depends on knowing what are the right questions to ask, when should they be asked, and who should answer them"; "tend to limit the investigation of new ideas"), semi-structured. Open vs closed questions appear under **questionnaires**, not interviews.
- On matching technique to situation: they cite Hickey & Davis's four reasons analysts actually choose ((a) only technique known, (b) favorite, (c) prescribed by methodology, (d) intuition), and close: "**requirements elicitation still remains more of an art than a science**." Ethnographic techniques are flagged as "very expensive… requir[ing] significant skill," with the observer effect noted.

### Hickey & Davis — the unified model of elicitation
- **Hickey, A.M. & Davis, A.M., "A Unified Model of Requirements Elicitation," *Journal of Management Information Systems* 20(4), Spring 2004, pp. 65–84, DOI 10.1080/07421222.2004.11045786.** ⚠️ Paywalled, no OA copy anywhere; not read directly. The model's content was verified from their HICSS'03 paper.
- **Hickey & Davis, "Requirements Elicitation and Elicitation Technique Selection: A Model for Two Knowledge-Intensive Software Development Processes," HICSS-36, 2003, DOI 10.1109/HICSS.2003.1174229** (read in full). The model: elicitation as iterated function application — **elicitᵢ(Rᵢ, Sᵢ, tᵢ) → Rᵢ₊₁, Sᵢ₊₁**, where Rᵢ = current knowledge of requirements, Sᵢ = situation (problem-domain + solution-domain + project characteristics), tᵢ ∈ T (all known techniques). Selection: **σ(Rᵢ, Sᵢ, χ(T)) → {applicable techniques}**, where χ(T) = static technique characteristics; then a personal selector **π({t}, P) → tᵢ** applying analyst preferences. Composed: elicitᵢ(Rᵢ, Sᵢ, π(σ(Rᵢ,Sᵢ,χ(T)),P)). A methodology is then just a fixed sequence of elicit steps — and their critique of methodologies is that fixing tᵢ a priori assumes Sᵢ and Rᵢ in advance: "one size [methodology] fits all."
- **Hickey & Davis, "Elicitation Technique Selection: How Do Experts Do It?", RE'03, pp. 169–178, DOI 10.1109/ICRE.2003.1232748** (read in full). In-depth interviews with nine named experts (Booch, Constantine, DeMarco, Gause, Lister, Lockwood, Robertson, Wiegers, Yourdon; the companion AMCIS'03 paper adds Reifer as tenth). Key finding: "although some experts think of themselves as practitioners of just one means of performing elicitation, they really have **a default means of doing elicitation, and anomalous conditions result in alternative approaches**." Four emergent constructs per technique: **Major Drivers, Anomalies, Prerequisite Skills, Success Enhancers**. They also self-apply Goguen & Linde: "A difference may exist between what these experts say they do, and what they actually do."
- The situational-characteristics ontology is in **Hickey & Davis, "A Tale of Two Ontologies: The Basis for Systems Analysis Technique Selection," AMCIS 2003, paper 386** (read in full): 24 printed coded situational characteristics in five categories (problem domain: FUZZ, CPLX, CNFL, MATU, SECU, RESP, SAFE, RELI; solution domain: TYPE, COTS, OUTS; stakeholders: #STK, STEX, STCM, STCP, STTV, STAC, STDV; solution builders: SOEX, SOCO, SOSW, SOTO; bridge-builders: BBEX, BBTE, BBCO) out of "over fifty" isolated; plus a **ten-dimension technique attribute vector** (physical co-location, temporal co-location, record-keeping, analyst role, convergence/divergence, anonymity, stakeholder count, tool-based, product/human focus, direct/indirect). Notable empirical observation: "a moderately good technique for a specific situation in the hands of an experienced 'master' can become an ideal technique for that situation." ⚠️ No numbered "propositions" exist in any of the three 2003 papers; if JMIS 2004 adds them, that is unverified.

### Goguen & Linde 1993 (read in full, author preprint)
**Goguen, J.A. & Linde, C., "Techniques for Requirements Elicitation," Proc. IEEE Int. Symposium on Requirements Engineering (RE'93), San Diego, 1993, pp. 152–164, DOI 10.1109/ISRE.1993.324822.** (Preprint: https://cseweb.ucsd.edu/~goguen/ps/rqelit.pdf. Affiliations: Goguen — Oxford PRG; Linde — Institute for Research on Learning.)

- Their categorization: traditional techniques (**introspection, questionnaires, interviews** [questionnaire interviews / open-ended interviews / focus & JAD groups], **protocol analysis**) vs discourse-analytic techniques (**conversation analysis, discourse analysis, interaction analysis**), grounded in ethnomethodology. Organizing principle: "The methods surveyed so far all impose an analyst's order on the social world, with no guarantee that this is the same as the order that members perceive."
- The **say–do problem**, verbatim: "People know how to do many things that they cannot describe… This problem is so familiar that it has a nickname in social science: the *say-do problem*; also, philosophers speak of *tacit knowledge*." Moral: "**Don't ask people to describe activities that they do not normally describe, or if you do, then don't believe the answers.**" Summary verdict: "**None of these methods can elicit tacit knowledge, and all are subject to the say-do problem.**"
- On introspection: "the introspection of an expert in a different field… is unlikely to reflect the experience of actual users"; "So called naive users are often experts in their own speciality, about which the requirements engineers are naive."
- On interviews: questionnaire interviews suppress "interactional resources that routinely mediate uncertainties of relevance and interpretation" (via Suchman & Jordan, JASA 1990); "Interview methods can fail if the interviewer and respondent do not share a category system"; elicited narratives formally differ from spontaneous ones (Wolfson).
- On matching to situation: they recommend a "**zooming**" method — cheap methods broadly, "the more expensive but detailed methods… only employed selectively for problems that have been determined by other techniques to be especially important"; recommended sequence: ethnography first, then interviews, then conversation/interaction analysis on selected hot spots. ⚠️ RE'93 contains **no table or figure**; the requirements-are-emergent claim ("requirements… gradually emerge from interactions") is verbatim only in Goguen's 1994 chapter "Requirements Engineering as the Reconciliation of Technical and Social Issues" (in Jirotka & Goguen, *Requirements Engineering: Social and Technical Issues*, Academic Press, 1994).

## 2. INTERVIEW MISTAKES — the Ferrari/Spoletini/Bano/Zowghi/Donati taxonomy (fully verified from author manuscripts)

### Chain of papers (all citations verified)
1. **Donati, B., Ferrari, A., Spoletini, P., Gnesi, S., "Common Mistakes of Student Analysts in Requirements Elicitation Interviews," REFSQ 2017, LNCS, pp. 148–164, DOI 10.1007/978-3-319-54045-0_11.** 36 students, 18 sessions; first set of **9 mistake categories** (full list paywalled; not verified verbatim).
2. **Bano, M., Zowghi, D., Ferrari, A., Spoletini, P., Donati, B., "Learning from Mistakes: An Empirical Study of Elicitation Interviews Performed by Novices," RE'18 (26th IEEE Int. RE Conf.), 2018, pp. 182–193, DOI 10.1109/RE.2018.00027.** (full text read via NSF PAR)
3. **Bano, M., Zowghi, D., Ferrari, A., Spoletini, P., Donati, B., "Teaching requirements elicitation interviews: an empirical study of learning from mistakes," *Requirements Engineering* 24(3), 2019, pp. 259–289, DOI 10.1007/s00766-019-00313-0.** (full author manuscript read; 110 students, 28 groups, role-played customer, two independent reviewers + customer think-aloud + observer)
4. **Ferrari, A., Spoletini, P., Bano, M., Zowghi, D., "SaPeer and ReverseSaPeer: teaching requirements elicitation interviews with role-playing and role reversal," *Requirements Engineering* 25(4), 2020, pp. 417–438, DOI 10.1007/s00766-020-00334-0** (full OA text read; note Ferrari-first author order). Conference precursor: Ferrari et al., "Learning Requirements Elicitation Interviews with Role-Playing, Self-Assessment and Peer-Review," RE'19, pp. 28–39, DOI 10.1109/RE.2019.00015.

### The complete taxonomy — 34 mistakes in 7 categories (Fig. 3 of the REJ 2019 paper, transcribed from the rendered figure, with frequency of occurrence in 28 groups)

**1. Question Formulation** — asking vague questions [21]; asking technical questions [15]; asking irrelevant/incorrect questions [6]; asking customer for solutions [4]; asking very long questions [1]; incorrect formulation of questions [1].
**2. Question Omission** — not identifying stakeholders [12]; no probing questions [11]; not asking about existing system/business process [11]; not asking about feature prioritisation [9]; not asking about the problem domain [7]; not identifying success criteria [1]; missing relevant questions [1].
**3. Order of Interview** — incorrect ending of interview [19]; incorrect opening of interview [15]; incorrect order of questions [15]; repeating the questions [6].
**4. Communication Skills** — unnatural dialogue style [14]; poor communication skills (language issues) [5]; poor listening skills [2]; voice of analyst (not clear) [1]; looking at their laptop during conversation [1].
**5. Analyst Behaviour** — lack of confidence [5]; overconfidence, arrogance [1]; passive attitude of analyst [1]; unprofessional behaviour [1].
**6. Customer Interaction** — no rapport with customer [16]; influencing customer [1]; interrupting customer [1].
**7. Teamwork and Planning** — lack of coordination and choreography among group [14]; lack of time management [3]; lack of preparation and planning to understand problem domain [2]; lack of planning (for interview) [1]; long pause during interview [1].

Most frequent overall: (1) asking vague questions, (2) incorrectly ending the interview (no final summary — 19/28 groups), (3) not building rapport (16/28). 12/28 groups failed to identify stakeholders.

Definitions worth quoting: vague questions = "the type of questions that may yield multiple interpretations, or cases where no reasonable meaning can be inferred from the questions asked." Recommended interview strategy in the study: build rapport → understand existing business process → understand problems in current process → summarize findings at the end to confirm understanding.

### The operationalized questionnaire (Appendix A, REJ 2019 — verbatim statements, 5-point Likert)
Question Formulation: "The analyst asked vague questions / asked technical questions / asked questions that appeared irrelevant to me / asked the customer for solutions / asked long and overly complex questions / formulated their questions in a way that appeared incorrect to me."
Question Omission: "The analyst DID NOT ask for additional stakeholders / DID NOT ask probing questions to confirm their understanding / DID NOT ask about the existing system or business process / DID NOT ask questions about feature prioritisation / DID NOT ask information about the problem domain / DID NOT identify goals and success criteria / DID NOT ask all the questions that I consider relevant."
Order of Interview: "DID NOT perform a summary at the end / started the interview by asking direct questions about the system / asked questions in an order that appeared incorrect to me / repeated the same questions multiple times."
Communication Skills: "dialogue style… appears unnatural / poor communication skills / poor listening skills / spoke with a low and unclear tone."
Analyst Behaviour: "showed lack of confidence / appeared overconfident or arrogant / showed a passive attitude / behaviour that appeared unprofessional."
Customer Interaction: "DID NOT create rapport with the customer / tried to influence the customer / interrupted the customer."
Teamwork & Planning: "lack of coordination and choreography among team members / did NOT manage their time / lack of preparation on the domain / did not plan the interview / long pauses during the interview."

### Longitudinal findings (Study 2, REJ 2019) and training effects (SaPeer, REJ 2020)
- Across three successive interviews, students **did not improve** in Question Formulation, Question Omission, or Interview Order; they got *worse* at omission in later interviews (failed to re-ask goals/success criteria of new stakeholders). No correlation found between interview performance and final SRS marks.
- SaPeer (role-play + mistake-based training + self-assessment + peer review): significant mistake reduction interview 1→2 (paired t = −4.77, p = 0.00012, Cohen's d = −1.037, large). Largest improvements: "no final summary," "no probing questions" — mistakes fixable by simple recommendations. Small/insignificant improvement: "not identifying success criteria," "not asking about feature prioritisation," and behavioural/systemic mistakes (Communication Skills, Analyst Behaviour, Customer Interaction) — "harder to correct with recommendations, and may require more exposure to practice." Improvement vs a practice-only control was **not** significant except for "asking customer for solutions" and "no final summary" — practice itself is the main active ingredient.

### Ambiguity as a cue in interviews (Ferrari/Spoletini line — read in full)
- **Ferrari, A., Spoletini, P., Gnesi, S., "Ambiguity and tacit knowledge in requirements elicitation interviews," *Requirements Engineering* 21(3), 2016, pp. 333–355, DOI 10.1007/s00766-016-0249-3** (postprint read). Based on 34 customer–analyst interviews. Central inversion: ambiguity is not just a defect but "**a resource for discovering tacit knowledge**" — each detected ambiguity is a trigger for a follow-up question; "every unidentified ambiguity is a missed opportunity." Formal framework built on Gervasi et al.'s tacit-knowledge predicates (articulated, interpretable, acceptable, accessible): **ambiguity ⇔ the customer articulated k, and the analyst either can't access k or interprets a different k′**. Four categories: **(1) Unclarity** (interpretation unclarity — jargon/inarticulate speech; acceptance unclarity — interpretation conflicts with the analyst's mental framework of goals/requirements/domain/specification), **(2) Multiple Understanding** (several acceptable readings), **(3) Incorrect Disambiguation** (single wrong interpretation; *detected* vs *undetected*), **(4) Correct Disambiguation** (a.k.a. innocuous ambiguity — resolved subconsciously, never surfaces). Quantitative sketch: with domain experts, interpretation unclarity 30% and domain-acceptance unclarity 21%; with CS-background customers, interpretation unclarity 24%, multiple understanding 24%, detected incorrect disambiguation 15%. Ambiguity disclosed tacit knowledge about requirements, goals, domain — and in some cases **unknown unknowns**.
- **Ferrari, Spoletini, Gnesi, "Ambiguity Cues in Requirements Elicitation Interviews," RE'16, pp. 56–65, DOI 10.1109/RE.2016.25.** Five categories of ambiguity **cues** (linguistic triggers a listening analyst should react to): **under-specified terms, vague terms, quantifiers, pronouns, domain-specific terms** (category list verified via secondary sources/abstract; full text paywalled).
- **Ferrari, Spoletini, Donati, Zowghi, Gnesi, "Interview Review: Detecting Latent Ambiguities to Improve the Requirements Elicitation Process," RE'17, pp. 400–405, DOI 10.1109/RE.2017.15**; and **Spoletini, Ferrari, Bano, Zowghi, Gnesi, "Interview Review: An Empirical Study on Detecting Ambiguities in Requirements Elicitation Interviews," REFSQ 2018, LNCS 10753, pp. 101–118, DOI 10.1007/978-3-319-77243-1_7** — a protocol in which a *reviewer* listens to the interview recording to flag ambiguities the interviewer missed, generating additional questions for the next interview.

### An independent synthesis: 28 interviewer-mistake criteria (Shen/Singhal/Breaux RE'25)
**Shen, Y., Singhal, A., Breaux, T.D., "Requirements Elicitation Follow-Up Question Generation," RE'25 (33rd IEEE Int. RE Conf.), 2025, pp. 117–129 (arXiv:2507.02858).** From 14 papers they synthesized **28 mistake criteria** in 7 concern areas; the 14 in-paper criteria (verbatim, each with source citations):
- *Follow-up questions:* fail to elicit tacit assumptions; fail to consider alternatives; no clarification when unclear; no clarification when contradictory; fail to elicit tacit knowledge.
- *Question framing:* ask a generic, domain-independent question; ask a question that is too long or articulated; use jargon; ask a technical question; ask a question inappropriate to user's profile; ask for solutions; ask a question that involves multiple kinds of requirements; ask a vague question that leads to multiple interpretations; ask a vague question which could infer no reasonable meaning.
The other 14 (from their artifact's references.pdf, verbatim): *Opening:* no guidelines; fail to build rapport; fail to understand the problem domain. *Ambience:* fail to be empathetic; fail to be ethical; no protection of privacy. *Elicitation goals:* fail to elicit explicit goals; fail to elicit feature priority; fail to elicit non-functional requirements. *Flow:* go back and forth among topics; interrogate the interviewee with too many direct questions; fail to analyze and react to the interviewee's response; interrupt in the middle of a response. *Closing:* fail to provide a summary.

## 3. Question taxonomies

### Zaremba & Liaskos 2021 — the RE-specific question typology (read in full)
**Zaremba, O. & Liaskos, S., "Towards a typology of questions for requirements elicitation interviews," RE'21 (29th IEEE Int. RE Conf.), 2021, pp. 384–389, DOI 10.1109/RE51729.2021.00042** (author PDF: yorku.ca/liaskos/Papers/RE2021/RE2021.pdf). Cross-disciplinary literature review yielding a typology on dimensions **Time / Content / Form / Style / Probing Style / Objective**, plus **sequencing patterns**. Selected verbatim rows (Fig. 1):
- **Time:** past / present / future.
- **Content:** users/stakeholders ("Who are the main users?"); needs/evaluations ("What are some issues with the current system?"); processes; objects/data; required functions ("Should the system allow you to…?"); technology; pragmatics ("What is your budget on…?").
- **Form:** open; bipolar; multiple choice; catchall ("Is there anything else that you think I should know about and we haven't yet discussed?"); comparison/contrast; declarative; introducing; specifying; structuring.
- **Style:** ready reference; instructional ready reference; directional; tour ("Could you explain to me the process step-by-step?"); targeted mini-tour.
- **Probing style** (>10 types): elaboration/informational ("Could you tell me more about that?"); reason-seeking ("Why do you think…?" — flagged as "an important tool for goal-oriented elicitation," citing Yu RE'97); clearinghouse ("What have I not asked that is important in this process?"); consistency ("You said earlier that ___ but then you told me ___. How do you explain that?"); interpreting; check-reflect; echo; question-reformulation; restatement; leading probe; silent probing; reflecting probe (counterfactual "what if the opposite were true").
- **Objective / content maneuvering** (bias-injecting forms): forced choice; leading; direct vs indirect; negative balance ("You seem to be very efficient. Do you remember of any occasions in which you had problems that slowed you down?").
- **Sequencing** (from Keats, *Interviewing*, 2000): simple structure; chain; branching with channeling; sequential with feedback loops; branching with complex loops; constellated. Consensus best practice: open/general questions first, specific later.
Related: Burnay, Jureta & Faulkner, "What stakeholders will or will not say…," *Information Systems* 46, 2014, pp. 61–81, DOI 10.1016/j.is.2014.05.006 (Elicitation Topic Map: six topic sets — items, rules, localization for scope; activities, connections, granularity for depth); Scheinholtz & Wilmont, "Interview Patterns for Requirements Elicitation," REFSQ 2011, pp. 72–77.

### Gause & Weinberg's context-free questions (verified against two independent scans of the book, Ch. 6, pp. 59–67)
**Gause, D.C. & Weinberg, G.M., *Exploring Requirements: Quality Before Design*, Dorset House, 1989, ISBN 0-932633-13-7.** Definition (p. 59): "high-level questions that can be posed early in a project to obtain information about global properties of the design problem and potential solutions… context-free questions help you decide which limb to climb, rather than which branch or twig."

**Context-free process questions** (§6.6, p. 65, verbatim): "Who is the client? / What is a highly successful solution really worth to this client? / What is the real reason for wanting to solve this problem? / Should we use a single design team, or more than one? / Who should be on the team(s)? / How much time do we have for this project? / What is your trade-off between time and value? / Where else can the solution to this design problem be obtained? / Can we copy something that already exists?"

**Context-free product questions** (p. 65, verbatim — the complete list is only four): "What problems does this product solve? / What problems could this product create? / What environment is this product likely to encounter? / What kind of precision is required or desired in the product?" (§6.3 wording says "this system" for the first three.)

**Metaquestions** (§6.4, pp. 62–64, verbatim): "Am I asking you too many questions? / Do my questions seem relevant? / Are you the right person to answer these questions? / Are your answers official? / …May I write down your answers and give you a written copy to study and approve? / Is there anyone else who can give me useful answers? / Is there someplace I can go to see the environment in which this product will be used?" End of every interaction: "Is there anything else I should be asking you? / Is there anything you would like to ask me? / May I return or call you with more questions later…?" Plus paired-interviewer nonverbal probes ("I notice that you hesitated a long time before answering… Is there something else we should know?").

**Verified heuristics elsewhere in the book:** the four ambiguity heuristics (§9.7, pp. 102–103): ambiguity poll; memorization heuristic; "Mary had a little lamb" (re-read emphasizing a different word each time); "Mary conned the trader" (mix-and-match dictionary definitions of key words). Ch. 25 "Ending" (pp. 277–284) is the book's answer to elicitation completeness: "Nobody can tell you just when to step off the cliff. It's simply a matter of courage" — with automatic design, hacking, freezing requirements, renegotiation, and fear of explicit assumptions named as the courage-avoiding pathologies. Rule of Three (~p. 211): "If you can't think of three things that might cause your great idea to fail, all that means is that you haven't thought enough about it yet."
⚠️ **Misattribution warnings:** "Reverse the process," "dropping the context," "Undiscovered Ruins," "Yes, but," and "Missing user" are **not** in this book — the last three are **Leffingwell & Widrig, *Managing Software Requirements*, Addison-Wesley, 1999, ch. 7** ("The search for requirements is like a search for undiscovered ruins — the more you find, the more you know remain…" — secondhand snippet, unverified against page). The "Rule of Three Interpretations" circulating in the testing community is not in this book either.

## 4. Contextual inquiry (Beyer & Holtzblatt)

**Citations:** Beyer, H. & Holtzblatt, K., *Contextual Design: Defining Customer-Centered Systems*, Morgan Kaufmann, 1998, ISBN 1-55860-411-1 (print date Sept 1997). 2nd ed.: Holtzblatt, K. & Beyer, H., *Contextual Design: Design for Life*, Morgan Kaufmann/Elsevier, 2017, ISBN 978-0-12-800894-2 (author order reverses). Also Holtzblatt, Wendell & Wood, *Rapid Contextual Design*, 2005. Primary verbatim sources actually verified: **Beyer & Holtzblatt, "Apprenticing with the Customer," *CACM* 38(5), 1995, pp. 45–52**, and **Holtzblatt & Beyer, *Contextual Design: Evolved*, Morgan & Claypool, 2014, §3.1**. Origin: **Whiteside, Bennett & Holtzblatt, "Usability Engineering: Our Experience and Evolution," in Helander (ed.), *Handbook of Human-Computer Interaction*, North-Holland, 1988, pp. 791–817** (confirmed as the authors' own cited origin); first named in Wixon, Holtzblatt & Knox, CHI'90, pp. 329–336.

**Four principles** (verbatim, 2014 formulation): **Context** ("While people do their life and work activities, observe and discuss what they are doing and why. Use artifacts… Use retrospective accounts—detailed re-telling of specific events in the recent past…"); **Partnership** ("let them lead the interview by doing their own activities and commenting on them… Do not come with planned questions"); **Interpretation** ("Determine the meaning of the user's words, emotions, and actions together with the user by sharing your interpretations… They will not let you mis-construe their motivations"); **Focus** ("Steer the conversation to meaningful topics… Let users know the focus so they can steer, too"). 1998 1st-edition wording is only partially verified (Context at p. 47 as "*ongoing experience* rather than *summary experience*, concrete data rather than abstract data"; Interpretation p. 58; Focus p. 62 — via a quotation site, treat as needing confirmation).

**Master/apprentice model** (CACM 1995, verbatim): "the relationship between master craftsman and apprentice stands out as a useful model… A master teaches by doing the work and talking about it while working." Five reasons it beats interviews: teaching ability not needed; **"People are not aware of everything they do… they become aware in the doing"** (footnoted to Polanyi); seeing work reveals details ("Talking about work while doing it protects the master craftsman from the human propensity to talk in generalizations… A design built on such generalizations may not meet anyone's needs… nothing is more detailed than an organization's software development policy manual, and there are few less reliable guides to how that organization actually develops software"); seeing work reveals structure; the apprentice learns from re-told recent events.

**What CI claims interviews away from the workplace cannot get** (2014, verbatim): "most people are not aware of what they really do. The everyday things people do become habitual and unconscious, so they are usually unable to articulate their practices… They are likely to forget about the workarounds they had to invent… If designers watch people while they engage in their activities, then people do not have to articulate their practices." Anti-abstraction technique: "If the customer says 'we usually get reports by email' ask, 'When was the last time that happened?'… Any statement in the present tense is usually an abstraction." Canonical example: the secretary who "just comes in and gets started" but enacts a fully structured routine when asked to do it. On bias: "customers are not easily led astray in the midst of their work… Open-ended questions give the customer less guidance in thinking about their work than an interpretation, and result in less insight."

**Critique/evaluation:** CI has essentially **no controlled empirical evaluation**. Davis, Dieste, Hickey, Juristo & Moreno, RE'06, pp. 179–188, DOI 10.1109/RE.2006.17 (systematic review): "**interviews, preferentially structured, appear to be one of the most effective elicitation techniques**; many techniques often cited in the literature… tend to be less effective than interviews." Dieste & Juristo, IEEE TSE 37(2), 2011, pp. 283–304: contextual techniques absent from aggregable evidence. Cost critiques: Zowghi & Coulin ("ethnographic techniques… very expensive"); Millen's "Rapid Ethnography" (DIS'00); the *Rapid Contextual Design* book itself. Epistemological critiques of field methods as requirements source: Anderson (HCI 9(2), 1994); Dourish, "Implications for Design" (CHI'06); Crabtree et al., "Ethnography Considered Harmful" (CHI'09).

## 5. Completeness — when is elicitation done?

**Standards (verbatim from the standards):**
- **IEEE Std 830-1998** §4.3.3: "An SRS is complete if, and only if, it includes… a) All significant requirements… b) Definition of the responses of the software to all realizable classes of input data in all realizable classes of situations… c) Full labels and references to all figures, tables… and definition of all terms and units of measure." §4.3.3.1: "Any SRS that uses the phrase 'to be determined' (TBD) is not a complete SRS." (Status: superseded by 29148:2011.)
- **ISO/IEC/IEEE 29148:2011** — individual requirement Complete: "needs no further amplification because it is measurable and sufficiently describes the capability…"; set-level Complete: "contains everything pertinent… In addition, the set contains no TBD/TBS/TBR clauses," with the NOTE: "include all requirements types; account for requirements in all stages of the life cycle; and involve all stakeholders in the requirements elicitation activity." Set characteristics 2011: Complete, Consistent, Affordable, Bounded.
- **29148:2018** — individual: Necessary, Appropriate, Unambiguous, Complete, Singular, Feasible, Verifiable, Correct, Conforming; set: Complete, Consistent, Feasible, Comprehensible, Able to be validated. Set Complete: "stands alone such that it sufficiently describes the necessary capabilities… without needing further information," plus the TBx rule.
- Observation: all definitions are circular or negative; the standards contain **no stopping rule**.

**Theory — completeness is undecidable/relative:**
- **Zave, P. & Jackson, M., "Four Dark Corners of Requirements Engineering," ACM TOSEM 6(1), 1997, pp. 1–30, DOI 10.1145/237432.237434** (read in full). All RE statements are about the environment; indicative (domain knowledge K) vs optative (requirements R); specifications S must satisfy **S, K ⊨ R**. Their §6.3 five completion criteria: R validated *informally* as "expressing all the customer's desires"; K validated *informally* as true of the environment; S properly restricted; proof S,K ⊨ R; proof S,K consistent. The completeness of R itself is irreducibly informal even in the strongest formal account. Also: "Because there is uncertainty about what [domain knowledge] is for, there is also uncertainty about how much of it to gather."
- **Leveson, N., "Completeness in Formal Specification Language Design for Process-Control Systems," FMSP 2000, pp. 75–87, DOI 10.1145/349360.351140** (read in full): "**Absolute completeness… is a theoretical but probably unattainable goal… the only truly complete specification of something would be the thing itself.** The trick is to determine whether a specification is *sufficiently* complete," relative to system requirements/hazard analysis. Her group's 26 → ~60 formal criteria trace to **Jaffe, Leveson, Heimdahl & Melhart, IEEE TSE 17(3), 1991, pp. 241–258, DOI 10.1109/32.75414** (read in full), whose robustness criteria are the formal counterpart of "asking about exceptions": every state must handle every possible input; the OR of transition guards must be a tautology; every state needs a timeout transition; all sensor inputs used; every legal output value producible. Decidable-side tooling: Heimdahl & Leveson, IEEE TSE 22(6), 1996, pp. 363–377. Empirical anchor: Lutz, RE'93 (DOI 10.1109/ISRE.1993.324825): safety-related errors arise mostly from requirements/interface incompleteness, not coding.
- **Zowghi, D. & Gervasi, V., "On the interplay between consistency, completeness, and correctness in requirements evolution," IST 45(14), 2003, pp. 993–1009, DOI 10.1016/S0950-5849(03)00100-9** (read in full): **internal** vs **external** completeness (after Cordes & Carver 1989; Boehm 1984 secondary); "it is impossible to define and measure absolute completeness… The notion of completeness is essentially related and tied to the purpose and goal of a system." Formally, S ∪ D ⊨ R assesses S's completeness w.r.t. R "but not the completeness… of R in isolation."
- **Firesmith, D., "Are Your Requirements Complete?", *Journal of Object Technology* 4(1), 2005, pp. 27–43** (verified): completeness disaggregated over seven objects; missing-element checklists per requirement type, including missing **exceptional "rainy day" paths** for functional requirements.

**Stopping rules / saturation:**
- The direct empirical answer to "when do interviewers stop": **Pitts, M.G. & Browne, G.J., "Stopping Behavior of Systems Analysts During Information Requirements Elicitation," *JMIS* 21(1), 2004, pp. 203–226, DOI 10.1080/07421222.2004.11045795**; precursors AMCIS 1998 (paper 265, read in full) and **Browne & Pitts, *OBHDP* 95(2), 2004, pp. 208–224**. Four cognitive stopping rules (after Nickles, Curley & Benson 1995): **magnitude threshold, difference threshold** ("stop… when he judges that he is no longer learning anything new" — saturation by another name), **mental list, representational stability**. Finding: "cognitive limitations of analysts result in flawed application… producing **premature termination**"; 54 professional analysts elicited only **57% of available requirement categories and 27% of available quality points** before stopping; procedural prompts help (**Pitts & Browne, ISJ 17(1), 2007, pp. 89–110, DOI 10.1111/j.1365-2575.2006.00240.x**).
- **Saturation is not used as an elicitation stopping rule in RE.** Origin: Glaser & Strauss 1967 (via Saunders et al., *Quality & Quantity* 52(4), 2018 — secondary); **Guest, Bunce & Johnson, "How Many Interviews Are Enough? An Experiment with Data Saturation and Variability," *Field Methods* 18(1), 2006, pp. 59–82, DOI 10.1177/1525822X05279903** — 70% of 114 themes in the first 6 interviews, 92% within 12 (numbers verified via Guest, Namey & Chen, PLOS ONE 2020, which also gives the only parameterised rule: base 4 + run 2, ≤5% new-information threshold). Full-text searches find **zero** RE papers proposing saturation as a project-level elicitation stopping criterion; capture-recapture (Petersson et al., JSS 72(2), 2004, pp. 249–264) is only ever applied to defects in an existing artifact — no one estimates never-elicited requirements. Sutcliffe & Sawyer, "Requirements elicitation: Towards the unknown unknowns," RE'13, pp. 92–104, DOI 10.1109/RE.2013.6636709 names the problem and leaves it open. Nielsen & Landauer's Found(i)=N(1−(1−λ)^i) model (INTERCHI'93, pp. 206–213; λ≈0.31; benefit/cost peak at 3.2 test users) has **no RE analogue**. Interestingly, ReqElicitGym (below) found effective elicitation questions are **back-loaded** in dialogues — the opposite of a decay curve.

**Goal-oriented RE as completeness driver:**
- **Yue 1987** (IWSSD-4) originated the criterion; **van Lamsweerde, "Goal-Oriented Requirements Engineering: A Guided Tour," RE'01, pp. 249–263, DOI 10.1109/ISRE.2001.948567** (read in full): "Goals provide a precise criterion for **sufficient completeness** of a requirements specification; the specification is complete with respect to a set of goals if all the goals can be proved to be achieved from the specification and the properties known about the domain." **R, As, D ⊨ g** for each goal g. Elicitation heuristics: "keep asking **HOW** questions" to refine, "**WHY** questions" to abstract — with the mechanism that a newly surfaced parent goal makes *missing sibling* subgoals discoverable. Formal AND-refinement completeness (entailment + minimality + consistency + non-triviality) is from **Darimont & van Lamsweerde, FSE-4, 1996, pp. 179–190, DOI 10.1145/239098.239131**: "intuitive refinements made 'by hand' tend to be incomplete. Incomplete goal refinements result in incomplete requirements."
- **van Lamsweerde & Letier, "Handling Obstacles in Goal-Oriented Requirements Engineering," IEEE TSE 26(10), 2000, pp. 978–1005, DOI 10.1109/32.879820** (read in full) — the formal counterpart of "asking about exceptions": obstacle O obstructs G iff {O, Dom} ⊨ ¬G and {O, Dom} ⊭ false; a set of obstacles is **domain-complete** iff **{¬O1,…,¬On, Dom} ⊨ G** — "completeness is a notion relative to what is known about the domain" (the aquaplaning example: whether the obstacle set is complete depends on whether the domain theory knows wheels can aquaplane). The **completion construction** O* = ◊(¬GC ∧ ¬OC1 ∧ … ∧ ¬OCk) literally manufactures the exception you haven't thought of. Identification: regression, proved obstruction patterns, and HAZOP-like heuristic checklists (InfoUnavailable, WrongBelief → InfoOutdated/InstanceConfusion/UnitConfusion…). Resolution taxonomy: elimination (goal substitution, agent substitution, obstacle prevention, goal deidealization, domain transformation), reduction, tolerance (goal restoration, mitigation, do-nothing). "Obstacle identification should be exhaustive for Safety or Security goals… cost-benefit analysis needs to be carried out to decide when the obstacle identification process should terminate." Later: **Alrajeh, Kramer, van Lamsweerde, Russo & Uchitel, "Generating Obstacle Conditions for Requirements Completeness," ICSE 2012, pp. 705–715** — automated generation found all the manually derived LAS obstacles **plus obstacles the authors' own manual formal analysis had missed**.
- **Yu, E., "Towards Modelling and Reasoning Support for Early-Phase Requirements Engineering," RE'97, pp. 226–235, DOI 10.1109/ISRE.1997.566873** (read in full) — i* explicitly **declines** the completeness framing for early RE: "the model of internal intentionality is not assumed to be complete… There is no definitive 'top'… nor 'bottom'… It is the stakeholders' decision as to when the issues have been adequately explored." What i* contributes to questioning is the **why-question battery** ("Why is it necessary to schedule meetings ahead of time?… And whose interests does it serve?") and vulnerability analysis over dependencies.
- Evidence that goal models improve completeness is thin: Horkoff et al., REJ 24(2), 2019, pp. 133–160 (mapping of 246 top-cited GORE papers: only ~7% controlled experiments); Mavin et al., "Does Goal-Oriented Requirements Engineering Achieve Its Goal?", RE'17, pp. 174–183, DOI 10.1109/RE.2017.40 (industrial use "inconsistent, informal, rarely… formal").
- **Convergent answer:** no source claims you can *know* elicitation is complete. Every serious treatment substitutes a relative criterion plus a **procedure that names its own gaps** — the failed refinement proof, the domain-consistent O*, the WHY question, the state with no timeout transition, Gause & Weinberg's "courage to end it all."

## 6. Elicitation by AI/LLM agents (2023–2026)

- **Korn, A., Gorsch, S., Vogelsang, A., "LLMREI: Automating Requirements Elicitation Interviews with LLMs," RE'25, pp. 19–30, DOI 10.1109/RE63999.2025.00013 (arXiv:2507.02564)** (read in full). GPT-4o chatbot, 33 simulated stakeholder interviews, evaluated **directly against the Bano et al. mistake questionnaire** (Question Formulation / Question Omission / Order of Interview / Communication Skills / Customer Interaction; 2 of the human categories dropped as inapplicable). Results: LLMREI made a similar number of mistakes to human interviewers; raters even scored its communication skills *better*; long "least-to-most" prompt embedding Ferrari et al. guidelines beat a 3-sentence zero-shot prompt on mistake avoidance (64.2% vs 59.1% disagree-mistake-present); elicited up to 60.9% of ground-truth requirements fully + 12.8% partially (73.7% total; ground truth = 12- and 8-requirement scenarios); ~50% of questions context-dependent. **What failed:** fine-tuning GPT-3.5 on 50 transcripts of Ferrari et al.'s student interviews failed entirely ("incoherent responses," lost focus — attributed to poor quality of novice-interviewer training data); hallucination (bot invented a project price estimate); boundary-crossing (asked for the user's email); and the bot **ended interviews too readily** when users signaled impatience, "missed opportunities to gather crucial information" — i.e., no stopping discipline.
- **Shen, Singhal & Breaux, RE'25 (arXiv:2507.02858)** (read in full; details in §2 above). Minimally guided GPT-4o follow-up questions were statistically indistinguishable from human interviewers'; **mistake-guided** generation (prompting with the 14 criteria) significantly *beat* human questions (68% preference; p < 1e-5 on relevancy). GPT-4o agreed with a human analyst on mistake classification 81% (340/420). Also derived a seven-type follow-up question typology from open coding of 146 real follow-ups: **topic change (71), answer probing (27), confirmation (13), question probing (13), alternative-seeking (11), preference-seeking (6), clarification (5)** — with topic-change questions needing no prior context and probing questions needing ≥2 turns.
- **Jin, D., Jin, Z., et al., "ReqElicitGym: An Evaluation Environment for Interview Competence in Conversational Requirements Elicitation," arXiv:2602.18306 (2026; under submission to TOSEM)** (read in full). 101 website scenarios, LLM oracle user + evaluator (κ = 0.72 vs experts); metrics **IRE** (implicit-requirements elicitation ratio), ESR, **TKQR** (nDCG-style, rewards early key questions). Findings: best LLM reaches **IRE 0.32**; models "overwhelmingly favor probing over clarification and **often lack effective stopping criteria**" (GPT-5.2 exhausts the 20-turn budget); effective questions emerge late; style-related requirements consistently missed.
- **Jin, D., et al., "From Chat to Interview: Agentic Requirements Elicitation with an Experience Ontology," arXiv:2605.05828 (RE 2026)** (read in full). OntoAgent: a three-level domain ontology (aspects → dimensions → clarification slots) induced from requirements corpora steers slot selection each turn (ParseUser/ScoreOnto/ReRankOnto/GatePrune); +33% IRE, +21% TKQR over free-form LLM chat, whose failure mode is "omission of implicit requirements and redundant questions."
- **Görer, B. & Aydemir, F.B., "Generating Requirements Elicitation Interview Scripts with Large Language Models," REW 2023, pp. 44–51, DOI 10.1109/REW57809.2023.00015**, and **"GPT-Powered Elicitation Interview Script Generator for Requirements Engineering Training," arXiv:2406.11439 (2024)** (read in full). Prompt-chained GPT script generation for training; expert evaluation found scripts coherent but **lacking depth** ("briefly touch on all these topics but lack depth," NFRs especially), and — assessed against the Donati/Bano mistake list — the generated scripts themselves exhibited **influencing the stakeholder, unnatural dialogue style, and ignoring other stakeholders** ("the interviewer does not ask for additional stakeholders in any of the scripts").
- **Ataei, M., et al., "Elicitron: A Large Language Model Agent-Based Simulation Framework for Design Requirements Elicitation," *J. Computing and Information Science in Engineering* 25(2), 2025, 021012 (arXiv:2404.16045)**: LLM agents *simulate users* (serial generation gave best persona coverage) and are interviewed by an LLM to surface latent needs — automation of the stakeholder side rather than the interviewer side.
- **Singhal, A., Carvalho, P., Breaux, T., "AI-assisted Script Management for Requirements Elicitation Interviews," arXiv:2608.01640 (2026)** (read in full). Full workflow: script generation from business goals under **9 literature-derived design rules** (goal alignment; introduction + rapport; **context-free opener** — Gause & Weinberg's term operationalized as a rubric rule; topic ordering; length; open-ended; one question per turn; clarity ≤ 30 words; concluding summary) + live topic-coverage tracking + on-demand follow-up generation. Untrained AI-assisted interviewers vs trained unassisted: AI scripts scored 92.8 vs 74.8/100; AI-assisted interviews covered **fewer topics** (9.6 vs 14.5) but more scripted questions (86% vs 69%) and 3× more follow-ups per topic — depth traded against breadth.
- Adjacent verified: Zowghi/Bano co-edited context; **Palomares et al., "The state-of-practice in requirements elicitation: an extended interview study at 12 companies," REJ 26(2), 2021, pp. 273–299**; Debnath & Spoletini's AnnoteREI (RE'22, pp. 255–256); **Ferrari, Spoletini, Debnath, "How do requirements evolve during elicitation?…" REJ 27(4), 2022, pp. 489–519, DOI 10.1007/s00766-022-00383-7** — only 30–38% of post-interview requirements trace fully to customer's initial ideas; requirements are "**co-created** through interviews," which reframes both the mistake and completeness literatures.

**Summary of what has been tried and failed in LLM interviewing:** fine-tuning on novice transcripts (failed outright); free-form chat (redundant, misses implicit requirements — IRE < 0.5 for all models); no model has a stopping criterion (either quits too eagerly on user impatience or exhausts the turn budget); style/NFR requirements systematically missed; hallucination and scope-creep remain live risks. What works: mistake-taxonomy-guided prompting (the Bano/Ferrari taxonomy has become the de facto evaluation instrument for machine interviewers), and structure injection (ontologies, scripts, topic trackers).
